# 6. The nature-in-industry firm: market, ethics and a feasibility check

This chapter answers four questions about part 2 of the plan (chapter 0.4), the part that gets most of the effort now. How big is the market? How does a small firm get into it? What are the ethical questions, and how does the firm answer each one? Is it feasible? Chapter 2 is the technical blueprint for the database; this chapter is the business and the ethics around it.

## 6.1 What the firm sells

Three services, from cheapest to deepest, plus two assets that grow with every project.

| Service | The client's question | What they get | Typical buyer |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Nature check** | "Is looking to nature worth it for this problem?" | A short, honest go or no-go: which biological strategies and nature-friendly options are relevant, how strong the evidence is, and whether it is worth a project. Sometimes the answer is no | Head of R&D or engineering |
| **Nature-friendly redesign** | "How do we make this product or process lighter on nature?" | Materials, energy, part design and process changes, each with a life-cycle assessment so the gain is measured rather than claimed | R&D, operations, product |
| **Biomimicry project** | "Can a biological mechanism solve this?" | Ranked mechanisms with evidence grades, a concept, and where useful a field study or bench test with a partner reserve or university | R&D |

**The two assets.**

1. **The database** (chapter 2), with a second layer added in 6.4: a map of where industry demand for nature-inspired solutions is concentrated.
2. **The reserve network:** protected areas and private estates near clients, under written agreements, where observation and field experiments happen and a fixed share of fees goes back.

The nature check is the door. It is cheap enough to say yes to, it shows the firm's honesty (a firm that sometimes says "no, use conventional engineering" is believed when it says yes), and a good share of checks should turn into one of the deeper projects [Guessing; measure the conversion rate from the first ten].

## 6.2 How big is the market

**Ignore the headline numbers.** Two kinds circulate, and neither describes this firm's market.

- **The biomimicry GDP figures.** "1.6 trillion dollars of global output by 2030" and "425 billion dollars of US GDP" come from the Fermanian Business and Economic Institute's 2010 and 2013 studies, commissioned by San Diego Zoo Global, which then promoted them. No peer review or independent review of the method was found, and its "Da Vinci Index" was openly questioned at the time [Da Vinci Index 2013; FBEI critique 2011, Likely]. The Biomimicry Institute still cited the 1.6 trillion figure in 2024 [Likely]. Do not put it in a pitch deck.
- **The biomimetic materials market.** Report vendors put it between about 20 and 80 billion dollars: 37.9 billion in 2020 rising to 65.9 billion by 2030 in one, 19.6 billion in 2020 to 26.1 billion in 2025 in another [AMR 2021; BCC 2020, Likely]. Definitions differ between vendors and probably include medical biomaterials [Guessing]. These are sales of materials, not spending on advice.

**The adjacent markets are real but large and general.** Global sustainability consulting was about 12.4 billion dollars in 2024, forecast near 30 billion by 2030; the same analyst cut its own earlier estimate by about 30 percent after redefining the market and because of regulatory uncertainty [Verdantix 2025, Likely]. No EU or Spain figure for sustainability consulting was found. All of Spain's consulting sector earned about 23.6 billion euros in 2025 [AEC 2026, Likely]. Bio-based polymers were 4.4 million tonnes in 2023, about 1 percent of fossil polymers, with capacity growing about 13 percent a year and Europe lagging [nova 2025, Likely].

**The number that matters is bottom-up.**

- Spain had 16,577 industrial companies with 20 or more employees and 8,889 construction companies of that size on 1 January 2025 [INE DIRCE 2025, Certain].
- About 5,700 industrial companies have 50 to 249 employees [Cifras PYME 2024, Likely], plus the large companies above them. Call it roughly 6,000 Spanish industrial companies big enough to have an R&D or engineering budget [Guessing, from the two figures].
- No published prices were found for biomimicry or life-cycle-assessment projects; Biomimicry 3.8 publishes none [Likely]. A working assumption: a nature check at a few thousand euros, a redesign or biomimicry project at 15,000 to 60,000 euros [Guessing].
- At 5 to 15 projects a year, that is roughly 75,000 to 900,000 euros of revenue [Guessing].

**What that means.** In years one to three the firm needs 10 to 40 paying clients out of about 6,000 Spanish candidates, before counting Portugal, France or the rest of the EU. That is under 1 percent of the home market. The limit on growth is how many projects a small team can deliver well, not how many companies could buy. The market is big enough; the question is whether the first clients will pay (6.8).

## 6.3 Where the demand comes from in 2026

Regulation drives much of what companies pay for. In 2026 the picture changed: the biggest reporting rules shrank, and the product and decarbonisation rules grew.

| Driver | What changed | Does it create paid work for this firm? |
|---|---|---|
| **Ecodesign for Sustainable Products Regulation** (EU 2024/1781) | The 2025 to 2030 working plan prioritises steel and aluminium, textiles, furniture, tyres and mattresses. Large companies may not destroy unsold clothing and footwear since 19 July 2026. Most product-specific rules were still pending in April 2026 [ESPR plan 2025; ESPR textiles 2026, Likely] | **Yes, growing.** Eco-design and life-cycle work for steel, aluminium and textiles, most likely from 2027 to 2028 as product rules land [Guessing] |
| **Corporate sustainability reporting (CSRD)** after the 2026 simplification | Adopted 24 February 2026: scope cut to companies with more than 1,000 employees and more than 450 million euros turnover [Omnibus I 2026, Likely] | **Much less than before.** Most Spanish mid-sized companies are out of scope |
| **Biodiversity reporting (ESRS E4)** | Required only where biodiversity is material, with many data points made voluntary [EFRAG E4 2025, Likely] | **Weak,** mainly large extractive, energy and infrastructure companies |
| **EU Taxonomy** | A 2026 act added a 10 percent materiality threshold and cut data points by about 64 percent [Taxonomy 2026, Likely] | **Less** |
| **Nature-related disclosure (TNFD)** | 802 adopters worldwide; Spanish adopters include Iberdrola, Acciona, Redeia and Ferrovial [TNFD 2026, Likely] | **Some,** voluntary and large companies only |
| **EU nature credits** | A 2025 roadmap: biodiversity certification first, tradeable credits later; pilots 2025 to 2027 [Nature Credits 2025, Likely] | **Not yet,** but the most relevant future channel for paying reserves (6.7.4) |
| **EU Bioeconomy Strategy** (November 2025) | Lead markets in construction materials, fibres, textiles and plastics; a company alliance to buy 10 billion euros of bio-based solutions by 2030; bio-based plastics targets due in 2027 [Bioeconomy 2025, Likely] | **Yes, from 2027,** for materials work |
| **Spain: industrial decarbonisation (PERTE)** | Line 1 awarded 518.2 million euros to 17 projects on 24 June 2026, for example 40.4 million to Alcoa's Spanish aluminium arm [PERTE DI 2026, Likely] | **Yes, now.** Large decarbonisation projects need engineering and grant-writing support |

**The reading.** Do not sell reporting. Sell product and process change: eco-design for the sectors the Ecodesign plan names, decarbonisation for companies with PERTE money, bio-based materials for the Bioeconomy lead markets. Biomimicry is the firm's difference, not its only product.

## 6.4 Where industry concentration lies, and why the database matters

Nobody has published a credible breakdown of bio-inspired patents by sector [research gap, Likely]. The best recent landscape study found 940 valid biomimicry patents from 1975 to 2021, half from the United States, with no sector split and internal inconsistencies in its counts [Bae 2023, Certain]. A broader keyword search finds about 13,000 granted patents [SPIE 2018, Likely]. Biomimetics publications grew from fewer than 100 a year in the 1990s to about 3,000 a year, concentrated in robotics, computer science and bioengineering [Lepora 2013, Likely]. No EPO, Fraunhofer or national patent office landscape was found.

Practitioners' client lists show where demand appears in practice [Likely]: construction and cities, cosmetics, energy and materials (Ceebios, France); aerospace, rail, energy, consumer goods and luxury (Bioxegy, France); car-making, automation and electrical enclosures (BIOKON members, Germany); steel (a Biomimicry Iberia co-founder works with ArcelorMittal).

**Most promising first buyers in Spain** [Guessing, from 6.3 and the client lists]: steel and aluminium, construction materials, packaging and cosmetics.

**So the database's second layer is a real gap, not a vanity project.** Map bio-inspired patents, publications, grants and practitioner projects by sector, problem type and region, alongside the mechanisms in chapter 2. It does three things:

1. It tells the firm where to sell, before anyone else knows.
2. It shows clients and investors that the firm knows the market, which is the reason you gave for building it.
3. It can be published in part as a public report, which is the firm's cheapest marketing.

## 6.6 How to get into the market

**1. Sell to the head of R&D, not the head of sustainability.** In the cases studied, companies run bio-inspired projects with their own R&D staff plus outside experts, and success came with top-down commitment, training and ambitious targets [Tempelman 2015; KPMG France 2023, Likely]. The barriers named in the research are perceived cost and risk, missing knowledge, regulation, and tension with how the business already works [Jones 2024; Mohr 2019, Likely]. The nature check answers all four: it is cheap, it is honest, it brings the knowledge, and it frames the change in engineering terms.

**2. Lead with work that already has money behind it.** Ecodesign compliance in steel, aluminium and textiles; decarbonisation for PERTE-funded projects; bio-based materials for the Bioeconomy lead markets. Biomimicry rides in as the differentiator inside these projects.

**3. Use grants to make the first projects cheap for clients.** Open or recurring in 2026 [all Likely; amounts mostly from secondary sources]:

- CDTI R&D projects (PID): about 550 million euros in 2025, open all year, minimum project size 175,000 euros; ENISA participative loans of 25,000 to 1.5 million euros for the firm itself [CDTI PID].
- LIFE programme: 601.5 million euros in 2026, up to 60 percent co-funding [LIFE 2026].
- Circular Bio-based Europe Joint Undertaking: 170.7 million euros in 2026 [CBE JU 2026].
- Horizon Europe Cluster 6: about 677 million euros in 2026 and 632 million in 2027 [Horizon C6].

The 2026 LIFE and CBE calls closed on 22 September 2026, so the 2027 rounds are the target; prepare consortium proposals in the first half of 2027.

**4. Partner, do not compete, at home.** Biomimicry Iberia (practitioners), Spanish universities (bench tests), the Spanish business and biodiversity initiative (IEEB, about 100 member organisations, already promotes land-stewardship agreements) [IEEB 2024, Likely], and EUROPARC-España (protected-area managers).

**5. Publish the market map.** A free annual "where nature-inspired innovation is concentrated in Iberian industry" report, built from the database's second layer, is the cheapest way to be known.

**A three-year shape** [Guessing; a plan to test, not a forecast]:

| Year | Focus | Target |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Ten nature checks, two to three paid projects, one reserve agreement, one university partner, first public market map | Proof that companies pay; validation cost per database record known |
| 2 | Ecodesign and bio-based work in two sectors; a grant-funded consortium project; second reserve | 8 to 15 projects; first repeat clients |
| 3 | Database as a paid product for repeat clients; Portugal or France | 15 to 40 clients; reserve payments published |

## 6.7 The ethics, and the firm's answer to each

Each question below ends with what the firm commits to. Together they are the firm's code (6.7.9), which should be published before the first client.

### 6.7.1 Greenwashing and "nature-washing"

**The risk.** Clients want to market products as "nature-inspired" or "nature-positive". Since 27 September 2026 the EU's Empowering Consumers Directive applies: it bans generic environmental claims ("eco-friendly", "nature-positive") without recognised excellent environmental performance, claims about a whole product that are true only of one part, sustainability labels without certification, and climate claims based on offsets [EmpCo 2024, Certain]. Spain has not finished transposing it; its sustainable consumption bill was approved at first reading in July 2025 [Consumo Sostenible 2025, Likely], and existing unfair-competition law already applies. The wider Green Claims Directive is stalled, not formally withdrawn [GCD 2025, Likely]. The UK regulator said in 2026 that everyone who makes, relays or relies on a green claim is responsible for it, logic that would reach a consultant [CMA 2026, Likely].

Biomimicry has its own history of overclaiming. The Eastgate Centre's termite ventilation story rests on a mistaken model of how mounds work [Turner and Soar 2008, Likely]. Speedo's shark-skin fabric did not reduce drag the way shark skin does [Oeffner and Lauder 2012, Likely]. A study of a lotus-effect paint found that copying nature does not make a product automatically more sustainable; it has to be measured case by case [Antony 2016, Likely].

**The firm commits to:** no environmental claim from any project without a life-cycle assessment (ISO 14040/44); never using "nature-positive" or other generic claims in client deliverables; separating "inspired by" (design history) from "better for nature" (needs evidence); never letting a client use a reserve's name as a label (Andalucía, for one, licenses the name and image of its natural parks [Marca Parque Natural, Likely]); professional indemnity insurance that covers claims advice.

### 6.7.2 Weapons and surveillance

**The risk.** Early biomimicry was "mostly funded by the military and space agencies", in Janine Benyus's words; she and Dayna Baumeister wrote an ethics code because "biomimicry can mean mimicking the shape of a penguin to make a torpedo" [Benyus 2018, Likely]. The US defence research agency put about 84 million dollars into more than 25 biomimetics projects from 1998 to 2004 [Likely]. EU export rules control "technical assistance" for dual-use items [Dual-use 2021, Likely]. No biomimicry consultancy with a published client-exclusion policy was found; advertising agencies have done it at scale (more than 1,000 signed the Clean Creatives pledge refusing fossil-fuel clients) [Clean Creatives 2024, Likely].

**The firm commits to:** a published exclusion policy before the first client (weapons, surveillance, fossil-fuel expansion), with a written test for dual-use cases; screening each engagement against the EU dual-use list; a contract clause forbidding reuse of field data or database content for weapons.

### 6.7.3 Fieldwork in protected areas

**The risk.** Disturbing wildlife is the most direct harm the firm could do. Spanish law requires an appropriate assessment for anything likely to significantly affect a Natura 2000 site, prohibits disturbing specially protected animals, and allows research exceptions only by a reasoned authorisation from the regional government [Ley 42/2007, Certain]. Regions add their own rules: in Extremadura, for example, any research that handles or disturbs species needs express, personal authorisation limited by species, dates and zones, and silence from the administration means no [Extremadura procedure, Likely]. EU animal-experiment law does not cover passive observation, but capture, marking or sampling may fall inside it [Dir 2010/63, Certain].

**The firm commits to:** non-invasive observation as the default; regional authorisation and the park director's sign-off for every field project; no fieldwork in breeding seasons for affected species; an independent ethics reviewer for any protocol that touches an animal; specimen collection only with both wildlife and genetic-resource authorisations (chapter 2.7).

### 6.7.4 Paying reserves without corrupting them

**The risk.** Money can bend a reserve's judgment: a park that depends on one industrial client may approve fieldwork it should refuse. Public bodies also cannot always accept earmarked private money. Since 2024 Spanish companies deduct 40 percent of donations to qualifying bodies (50 percent for repeat donations) [RDL 6/2023, Certain], and business collaboration agreements allow funding in exchange for public mention only [Ley 49/2002, Likely]; a fee share tied to field access is payment for a service, not a donation, and needs its own contract [Guessing; confirm with a lawyer]. Much of the dehesa is private: only about 7 percent of Extremadura's forest land is public [Extremadura forests 2021, Likely], so the right recipient may be a landowner or a land-stewardship body rather than a park. Spain had 4,632 land-stewardship agreements by 2023 [Custodia 2025, Likely]. Voluntary payment schemes for nature fail: the UN's Cali Fund had about 6,000 dollars after a year and a half [Cali Fund contributions, Likely].

**The firm commits to:** a fixed percentage of every project fee written into the client contract, never voluntary and never dependent on findings; payment into a ring-fenced fund held by a foundation or land-stewardship body, never to individuals; a cap on any single client's share of a reserve's budget; a binding veto for the reserve over any field protocol; publishing all payments every year; naming the payment a conservation contribution, not benefit-sharing (chapter 2.7). When EU nature credits arrive (from about 2027), test whether the fund can certify what it pays for.

### 6.7.5 Shepherds, farmers and local knowledge

**The risk.** Much of what is known about how a dehesa works lives with the people who work it. Transhumance is UNESCO intangible heritage since 2023 in a nomination led by Spain [UNESCO 2023, Likely]. Spanish law tells public bodies to promote fair sharing of benefits from traditional knowledge but does not expressly require the holders' consent [Ley 42/2007, Certain]. International guidelines treat consent as including the right to refuse, and as ongoing [Mo'otz Kuxtal, Likely].

**The firm commits to:** treating shepherds, farmers and rangers as named contributors; written consent that includes the right to refuse; a share agreed in advance, in money or co-authorship; credit in the database; quarantining any record that comes from traditional knowledge until consent is in place (chapter 2.7).

### 6.7.6 Who owns what is invented

**The risk.** A discovery of how nature works cannot be patented, but a technical application of it can [EPC Art. 52, Likely]; in Europe, plant and animal varieties are excluded, and so are plants and animals obtained only by essentially biological processes [G 3/19, Likely]. Without a contract, ownership falls to whoever invents [Guessing]. A database can be protected by the EU database right [Likely].

**The firm commits to:** contracts stating that the client owns its specific invention, the firm keeps its methods and the database, and the reserve receives a royalty or milestone share if a product reaches market; an open core (descriptions of biological mechanisms published openly with attribution) and proprietary demand data and market map.

### 6.7.7 An AI-built database

**The risk.** A model can invent a mechanism, and a client can build on it. Commercial text-and-data mining in the EU relies on an exception that rightsholders can opt out of; a German court ruled in 2025 on what counts as a valid opt-out [Kneschke 2025, Likely]. Under the EU AI Act, a firm using existing models has light obligations, mainly AI literacy [AI Act delay 2026, Likely].

**The firm commits to:** respecting every opt-out and robots rule when collecting literature; keeping the source and the verbatim supporting sentence for every record (chapter 2.3); a biologist's verification before any record is marked validated; disclaimers and insurance on engineering advice.

### 6.7.8 The word "biomimetic" itself

**The risk.** "Natural equals good" is a fallacy [Estadieu 2025, Likely], and biomimicry's promises depend on how it is practised [MacKinnon 2020, Likely]. Two standards define the method: ISO 18458 (terminology and method; a product is "biomimetic" only if it went through that process) and Germany's VDI 6220, whose first part covers biomimetics and sustainability [ISO 18458; VDI 6220, Likely].

**The firm commits to:** using the word "biomimetic" only for work that meets ISO 18458 and VDI 6220.

### 6.7.9 The code, in one list

1. No environmental claim without a life-cycle assessment.
2. No generic "nature-positive" language; "inspired by" is not "better for".
3. No weapons, surveillance or fossil-fuel expansion clients.
4. Non-invasive fieldwork by default, always authorised, never in breeding season.
5. A fixed share of every fee to a ring-fenced reserve fund, published yearly, with a reserve veto.
6. Local knowledge credited, consented and shared.
7. Clients own their inventions; the firm owns its methods; reserves share in products that reach market.
8. Every database record sourced, quoted and verified before it is called validated.
9. "Biomimetic" only by the standard.
10. Say no when nature is not the answer.

## 6.8 Quick feasibility check

| Question | Verdict | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Is there a market? | **Yes, small and enough** | About 6,000 Spanish industrial candidates; 10 to 40 clients is under 1 percent of them (6.2) |
| Is there paid demand now? | **Yes, but not for biomimicry alone** | Real money flows to eco-design and decarbonisation (Ecodesign Regulation, PERTE); reporting-driven demand shrank in 2026 (6.3) |
| Is there room against competitors? | **Yes** | No commercial firm in Spain combines biomimicry, a market map and reserve payments; the nearest models are French and German, partly publicly funded (6.5) |
| Does the reserve payment work? | **Only as a contract line** | Voluntary nature payments fail (Cali Fund); a fixed fee share in every contract does not depend on goodwill (6.7.4) |
| Is the database worth building? | **Yes, as a second layer** | The sector map is a documented gap; the mechanism library becomes valuable only once validated (6.4; chapter 2) |
| Is it ethically workable? | **Yes, with the code published first** | Every risk in 6.7 has a known answer; the dangerous ones (greenwashing, weapons, disturbance) are avoided by refusing, not by managing |
| What limits it? | **People, not market** | Delivery capacity and validation time; the first hire signs reserve agreements and sells to R&D heads (chapter 5.3, risk 10) |
| Could it make a lot of money? | **Not as pure consulting** | Consulting at this scale is a few hundred thousand to a few million euros a year. The upside is the database as a product and the link to Sylva's farms (bio-based materials) |

**Verdict.** Feasible as a small, honest consultancy that leads with fundable eco-design and decarbonisation work, uses biomimicry as its difference, pays reserves through contracts and builds the market map as it goes. Not feasible as a pure biomimicry business that waits for companies to pay for inspiration, and not a source of large revenue in the first three years. The pure-commercial model is unproven: the best-known European players are partly publicly funded or cooperative [Ceebios 2023, Likely], so plan for grants in years one and two.

**What would make you stop** (in the spirit of chapter 5.4):

- Fewer than two of the first ten nature checks turn into a paid project.
- Validation costs more than an hour per database record after the second project (chapter 5.4).
- No reserve or land-stewardship body will sign an agreement with a fixed share and a veto within six months.

## 6.9 Questions only you can answer

1. **One company or two?** Is the firm "the second part of Sylva" in one company, or its own company that partners with Sylva? One company shares the reserve and dehesa relationships; two keeps the farmer brand separate from industrial clients.
2. **How broad to start?** Biomimicry plus eco-design from day one, or biomimicry first and eco-design added later? The evidence in 6.3 says start broad.
3. **Where is the line on clients?** The code excludes weapons, surveillance and fossil-fuel expansion. Do you also exclude, for example, fast fashion or single-use plastics, or work with them to change?
4. **How open is the database?** The suggestion is open mechanism descriptions and a proprietary market map. Is that the balance you want?
5. **Who is the first hire?** Someone who sells to R&D heads and signs reserve agreements, or a biologist who validates records?
