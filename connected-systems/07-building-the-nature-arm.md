# 7. Building the nature arm: the step-by-step plan

This chapter is the plan for creating part 2 of the project: the arm of Sylva that helps industry look to nature first. Chapter 6 asked whether it can work and what the ethics are; this chapter says what to do, in what order, and how to know each step is done. It is a plan, not a technical specification.

Written 2026-10-10. Tags as in the other chapters; most of this chapter is intention, not finding, and is untagged.

## 7.1 What has been decided

These were settled on 2026-10-10 and the rest of the plan is built on them:

1. **It is part of Sylva.** One company, one brand: Sylva works with land, and this arm works with industry. They share the same reserves, the same landscapes, the same honesty rules and the same pipeline for reading literature into records (chapter 2.2).
2. **The scope is as broad as possible.** Not only biomimicry. Anything that makes a product, or the way it is made, work more like nature (7.2).
3. **Two databases, both completely open.** One maps what nature knows; the other maps where industry is doing nature-inspired R&D, sector by sector (7.3).
4. **No client is excluded.** The arm works with any sector, including defence and security. What it guards is the honesty of its claims and the protection of the places it works in, not the identity of its clients (7.4).
5. **The first hire is not decided yet.** The plan makes it a decision point, taken when the first projects show where the bottleneck is (step 9).

## 7.2 The scope: everything that makes industry work more like nature

Seven areas. A client can come in through any one of them, and most projects will touch several.

| Area | The question it answers | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Form** | Can a shape or structure from nature do this job better? | Light, strong lattices modelled on bone or plant stems; surfaces that shed water and dirt |
| **2. Process** | Can we make this the way nature makes things: at ambient temperature and pressure, in water, without toxic inputs? | Adhesives, coatings and pigments made with lower energy and safer chemistry |
| **3. Materials** | Can a bio-based, recycled or regenerative material replace this one? | Cork, wood, natural fibres, mycelium; materials from the farms Sylva helps transition |
| **4. Energy** | Can this use less energy, the way living systems run on what is around them? | Passive cooling and ventilation; low-friction surfaces; heat recovery |
| **5. Circularity** | Can this be designed so nothing is wasted, as in an ecosystem? | Design for repair, disassembly and reuse; one factory's waste as another's input |
| **6. Systems** | Can the site or supply chain work like an ecosystem? | Industrial symbiosis between neighbouring plants; water cycles on site; local sourcing |
| **7. Place** | Can the company's land and surroundings support nature rather than replace it? | Nature-based solutions on industrial sites; partnerships with nearby reserves |

The rule that holds it together: **nature is the first place to look, not the only answer.** Every project starts with a nature check (chapter 6.1), and the arm says so when conventional engineering is the better answer.

## 7.3 The two open databases

### The nature library

What nature knows that industry could use. One record per mechanism or practice, not per species (chapter 2.3), covering all seven areas: a biological mechanism (how the lotus leaf stays clean), a nature-based material (what cork does and where it comes from), or a nature-friendly process (how a material is made at room temperature). Each record says where the knowledge came from, how strong the evidence is, and whether a person has checked it (`ai_extracted`, `expert_validated`, `disputed`). Records from partner reserves and from local knowledge credit the place and the people.

### The industry R&D map

Where industry is doing nature-inspired and nature-friendly R&D, by sector. Patents, research papers, public grants, published projects and companies, sorted by sector, problem type and region. No credible map like this exists today (chapter 6.4), so this database is new to the world, not just new to the arm.

### The link between them

The value is in the connection: an industry problem in the map points to the mechanisms in the library that could solve it, and a mechanism in the library shows which sectors are already using it and which are not. The gaps (a strong mechanism nobody in a sector has tried) are where the arm sells.

### What "completely open" means

- **Anyone can read, download and reuse both databases,** including competitors and commercial users. The suggested licence is Creative Commons Attribution (CC BY 4.0): free for any use, as long as the source is credited, which keeps the credit to reserves and local contributors that chapter 6.7.5 promises. The fully public-domain alternative (CC0) is more open but drops the credit.
- **Only what can be shared goes in.** Facts can be shared freely; long quotations from papers cannot. The verbatim supporting sentence on each record (chapter 2.3) is taken only from openly licensed papers; for others, the record cites the paper and page instead [Guessing on the exact line; confirm with the lawyer in step 3].
- **Nothing from Sylva's current species data goes in until the licence question is answered.** Sylva's 611 species records were extracted from a database with an all-rights-reserved notice (chapter 1.9 and the main report). Publishing them openly would make that problem public. Fix the licence first.
- **Clients' confidential problems never go in.** The databases hold what is public. What a client asked, and what the arm found for them, stays private unless the client agrees to publish it as a case study.

### Why open is the right call

- **Trust.** A firm whose evidence anyone can check is easier to believe than one that sells a black box.
- **Funding.** EU research funding expects open science, so open databases make the arm a stronger grant partner [Likely].
- **Reach.** Universities, practitioners and reserves can add records, so the library grows faster than the arm could grow it alone.
- **The moat moves, it does not disappear.** What competitors cannot copy is the people, the speed, the judgement, the validated records arriving first, and the reserve network. The databases bring clients to the door; the services are what they pay for.

## 7.4 Open to every client: what that commits the arm to

Excluding no client is your decision, and the plan takes it as given. Four things follow from it that the plan has to handle.

1. **The law still sets a floor.** Some work for defence and security is regulated whatever a firm's policy is. EU dual-use export rules control technical assistance related to listed items [Dual-use 2021, Likely], and Spain controls trade in defence material separately [Guessing on the specific procedures; confirm with the lawyer]. The arm screens every project against these rules and does not do work that needs a licence it does not hold.
2. **Some funding is civilian only.** Horizon Europe funds only civil applications [Likely; confirm in the programme rules], so a defence project cannot be part of a Horizon proposal. The European Defence Fund exists for defence research [Likely]. Keep defence and civil projects in separate funding streams.
3. **Partners decide for themselves.** A reserve, a landowner, a university or a shepherd may not want their land, staff or knowledge used for a weapons project. They keep the right to say no. Before any partner agrees to a field project, the arm tells them which sector the project serves. That keeps the reserve veto in chapter 6.7.4 meaningful.
4. **The Sylva brand is shared.** Because this arm sits inside Sylva, farmers and reserves will see its clients as Sylva's clients. Decide in advance how you will explain the open-to-all policy to them; a short public statement of the policy is better than being asked about it later.

What stays exactly as before: no environmental claim without a life-cycle assessment, non-invasive and authorised fieldwork, a fixed reserve share in every contract, credited local knowledge (chapter 6.7). The arm does not judge clients; it judges claims and protects places.

## 7.5 The plan in ten steps

The steps overlap. Months are counted from the day you start; they are targets to test, not forecasts.

### Step 1. Set up the arm inside Sylva (month 1)

- **Do:** name the arm and decide how it appears on Sylva's site and documents. Confirm Sylva's legal form can carry consulting contracts (if Sylva is not yet a company, incorporate it, for example as a Spanish limited company). Open separate accounts for the arm's projects and for the reserve fund.
- **Output:** a name, a page, a legal entity, two accounts.
- **Done when:** the arm can sign a contract and receive payment.

### Step 2. Write the charter (month 1)

- **Do:** one public page that says what the arm is and how it works: the seven areas (7.2); the two open databases and their licence (7.3); the open-to-all client policy and the legal floor (7.4); the claims rule (no environmental claim without a life-cycle assessment); the fieldwork rule (non-invasive, authorised, never in breeding season); the reserve rule (a fixed share of every fee to a ring-fenced fund, published yearly, with a veto for the reserve); the local knowledge rule (credited, consented, shared); the word "biomimetic" used only by the ISO 18458 standard.
- **Output:** the charter, published.
- **Done when:** a stranger can read it in five minutes and know what the arm will and will not do.

### Step 3. Get the legal groundwork done once (months 1 to 3)

- **Do:** one lawyer, one package of work: the client contract (ownership of inventions, no-reliance on engineering advice, claims rule); the reserve agreement (fee share, fund, veto, sector disclosure); the database licence and what can be quoted; the dual-use and defence-trade screening procedure; the genetic-resources edge cases (chapter 2.7); professional indemnity insurance.
- **Output:** four templates, one screening checklist, one insurance policy.
- **Done when:** the first client and the first reserve can sign without a new legal question.

### Step 4. Build the first industry R&D map (months 1 to 4)

- **Do:** start with Spain and Portugal and eight to ten sectors (start with the ones chapter 6.4 names as promising: steel and aluminium, construction materials, packaging, cosmetics, plus textiles, automotive, energy and agrifood). For each, collect bio-inspired and nature-friendly patents, papers, public grants, published projects and the companies behind them, from open sources.
- **Output:** the map, version 0, published openly, plus a short public report: "Where nature-inspired innovation is concentrated in Iberian industry."
- **Done when:** for each sector you can name the main problems being worked on, the companies working on them, and the gaps.

### Step 5. Seed the nature library (months 2 to 5)

- **Do:** use the map to pick the two or three sectors with the biggest gaps. Fill the library with 100 records for those sectors, across all seven areas, from openly licensed literature, using the pipeline Sylva already has (chapter 2.2). Add the species and materials of the first partner landscape, for example the dehesa's cork oak, holm oak and grazing system.
- **Output:** the library, version 0, published openly, with its coverage statistics on the front page (how many records, how many validated, how many with measured values).
- **Done when:** a client question in one of the chosen sectors returns at least five relevant records with sources.

> **Build status (2026-10-10).** Version 0 of both databases and the tool that searches them are built and in the repository. This is software and seed data, not the finished step: nothing in the library is expert-validated yet.
>
> - **Library:** 109 records, 100 served and 9 held back (8 biochemical or genetic traits waiting on legal review and 1 traditional-practice record waiting on consent; the serving gate is in `app/services/nature/store.py`). Provenance is `ai_extracted` for 96 served records and `disputed` for 4. Four records carry a measured value. 153 of 154 DOIs verify against Crossref. The library was extended on 2026-10-11.
> - **Map:** 502 papers and 5 companies in Spain and Portugal across ten sectors, harvested from OpenAlex. Only about 31% of papers carry a problem label, because the labels come from keywords and precision was favoured over recall (about 22% of the labels are estimated doubtful). Sector gaps are therefore computed from classified papers only, and the unclassified count is shown beside them.
> - **Search and answers:** `app/services/nature/` and `app/routers/nature.py`, with a `/nature` page in `frontend/src/Nature.jsx`. Ranking is the published formula plus two rules: records matching the challenge's parsed functions rank first, and records carrying those functions are added even when the text does not match, which is how Spanish and Portuguese challenges find the English records. The model only phrases the answer and can cite only retrieved ids. Without `GEMINI_API_KEY` the answers are a deterministic fallback; the embedding and model paths are tested with mocks only.
> - **Pipeline:** `scripts/nature/` holds the validator, the DOI checker, the OpenAlex harvester, the index builder, the extraction pipeline and the review tool.
> - **Not yet done:** the "at least five relevant records" check passes on five test challenges, not on client questions. Expert validation, patents and grants in the map, and links between map items and library records are still to do. The datasheet and known gaps are in `data/nature/README.md`.

### Step 6. Sign the first partners (months 2 to 6)

- **Do:** one reserve or land-stewardship body (much of the dehesa is private, so a private estate or a stewardship organisation may be the first, not a park [Extremadura forests 2021, Likely]); one university group with a biologist and an engineer, for validation and bench tests; introductions to Biomimicry Iberia, the Spanish business and biodiversity initiative (IEEB) and EUROPARC-España. Use Sylva's existing relationships with farmers and landscapes first.
- **Output:** one signed reserve agreement, one university agreement, three introductions.
- **Done when:** a client project could include fieldwork and a bench test next month.

### Step 7. Run the first ten nature checks (months 4 to 8)

- **Do:** offer the nature check (a short go or no-go on whether looking to nature is worth it for a given problem) to ten companies in the chosen sectors, found through the map. Charge a small fee or offer the first ones free in exchange for a published case study. Sell to heads of R&D and engineering (chapter 6.6).
- **Output:** ten written nature checks, each with a clear yes or no.
- **Done when:** ten are delivered and you know how many clients want to go further.

### Step 8. Deliver the first paid projects (months 6 to 12)

- **Do:** turn two or three nature checks into paid projects. Aim for one of each kind: a nature-friendly redesign with a life-cycle assessment; a biomimicry project; and, if a partner agrees, one with fieldwork at the reserve. Pay the reserve share on the day the client pays.
- **Output:** two or three delivered projects, the first reserve payment, two published case studies.
- **Done when:** at least one client says, in writing, that they would buy again.

### Step 9. Measure, then decide the first hire (month 9)

- **Do:** look at where time went in steps 4 to 8, and hire for the bottleneck the evidence shows:

| If the bottleneck was | Hire |
|---|---|
| Finding and convincing clients | Someone who sells to heads of R&D and engineering |
| Checking records and evidence | A biologist who validates records and runs fieldwork with reserves |
| Doing the technical work | An engineer or life-cycle assessment specialist |
| Signing partners and running agreements | Someone who builds relationships with reserves, landowners and universities |

- **Output:** a job description written from evidence, not guesswork.
- **Done when:** the first hire starts.

### Step 10. Fund it and make it repeatable (months 6 to 18)

- **Do:** apply for the arm's own early funding (CDTI and ENISA are open all year [CDTI PID, Likely]). Build consortium proposals with clients and the university partner for the 2027 rounds of LIFE, the Circular Bio-based Europe partnership and Horizon Europe Cluster 6 [LIFE 2026; CBE JU 2026; Horizon C6, Likely], keeping defence work out of civil-only proposals (7.4). Turn the services into a price list. Publish version 1 of both databases and the second yearly map report. Sign a second reserve and add a second region.
- **Output:** at least one grant submitted, a price list, database version 1, a second reserve.
- **Done when:** a new client can be served without inventing anything for them.

## 7.7 The checkpoints

Three moments to stop and decide, with the evidence that decides them (from chapter 6.8):

| When | Continue if | Rethink if |
|---|---|---|
| Month 8, after step 7 | At least two of the ten nature checks want a paid project | Fewer than two do: the offer, the sectors or the buyer is wrong |
| Month 12, after step 8 | Validating a database record takes under an hour, and a client would buy again | Validation takes longer, or no client would return |
| Month 6 and again at month 12 | A reserve or stewardship body has signed with a fixed share and a veto | Nobody will sign: the reserve model needs a different partner or structure |

## 7.8 What this arm needs from the rest of Sylva

1. **The pipeline.** Sylva's literature-to-records pipeline, with a new schema for mechanisms (chapter 2.2).
2. **The provenance flag.** Built for the library first and then added to Sylva's own species data (chapter 1.9).
3. **The licence fix.** Settle the Agroforestree licence before anything from Sylva's species data enters an open database (7.3).
4. **The landscapes and relationships.** Sylva's farmers and pilot region are the arm's first reserves, landowners and materials.
5. **The materials link.** Farms in transition grow bio-based materials (cork, wood, fibres) that the arm's clients can use, which gives farmers a second market and the arm a local supply (chapter 0.7).

## 7.10 The open catalogue, as built

Written 2026-10-12. This is a finding about what was built, not a plan.

The catalogue is a third open dataset beside the library and the R&D map: 99,309 records of papers, EU projects and adaptation case studies about learning from nature, sorted by industry sector and published at `/catalogue`. It was built to be big and free first, and checked second, which is the opposite order from the library (7.3).

**Why it has no RAG.** The library answers questions, so it needs retrieval. The catalogue only has to be browsed and downloaded. A person picks a sector and reads or saves it. Leaving out embeddings and a model means the build is the same every time (publishing twice gives identical bytes), it needs no key and no running cost, and nobody has to trust a model's idea of what a record is about.

**Rules-only sorting, and what the spot-check says.** Each record is scored against every sector using curated code tables (patent classes, OpenAlex subfields, EU science vocabularies) and keyword lists in English and Spanish, and it goes to the highest score. Records with no clear signal go to `other`, and a build fails if `other` grows past a quarter. Independent AI agents then checked blind samples of 200 records. After tuning, strict precision was 62.8%, lenient 74.9% and relevance 78.7%. A person has not yet checked it: the label on the page and in the manifest is "agent, owner skim pending", and it stays that until the owner skim is done. Several sectors fall below 70% strict precision and carry a warning on the page. Basic science with no application is the commonest error.

**Donation links go to the groups' own pages.** About 14,000 records name an organism or habitat, and rules match them to 49 conservation groups. Each button opens the group's own donation page. Sylva does not handle money, takes no share and does not claim a partnership with any group. The arm may point at the people who protect a place, but it does not stand between a donor and them.

**The honest gaps.** Not every nature-based-solution keyword has been harvested yet (110 of 288 jobs pending, because of a daily limit on the free tier). Patents are missing until a USPTO key exists. About 85% of records name no habitat or organism and so carry no donation link. Only 15,319 records carry a summary, because a summary is stored only when its source licence is CC BY or CC0; the rest show the title, the link and the sorted facts.
